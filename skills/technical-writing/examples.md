# Technical Writing — Examples

All examples are from William Zinsser, _On Writing Well_ (25th anniversary
edition, 2001), and _Writing to Learn_ (1988), unless noted.

---

## 1. Simplicity: Roosevelt rewrites a government memo

The original 1942 blackout order:

> Such preparations shall be made as will completely obscure all Federal
> buildings and non-Federal buildings occupied by the Federal government during
> an air raid for any period of time from visibility by reason of internal or
> external illumination.

Roosevelt's rewrite:

> Tell them that in buildings where they have to keep the work going to put
> something across the windows.

**What it shows.** The original isn't _wrong_ — it's just inflated. Same
information, half the words, no Latinate fog, a living human voice. Always ask:
what am I actually telling someone?

---

## 2. Clutter: Orwell rewrites Ecclesiastes

The King James original:

> I returned and saw under the sun, that the race is not to the swift, nor the
> battle to the strong, neither yet bread to the wise, nor yet riches to men of
> understanding, nor yet favor to men of skill; but time and chance happeneth to
> them all.

Orwell's parody, written in modern bureaucratic English:

> Objective consideration of contemporary phenomena compels the conclusion that
> success or failure in competitive activities exhibits no tendency to be
> commensurate with innate capacity, but that a considerable element of the
> unpredictable must invariably be taken into account.

**What it shows.** The second version has more _syllables_ but less _meaning_.
Short Anglo-Saxon words (race, battle, bread, riches) carry weight; abstract
Latinate words (consideration, phenomena, capacity) deflect it. When a sentence
sounds important, check whether it's saying anything.

## 3. The lead: hooking the reader with a single odd fact

Zinsser's own lead from "Block That Chickenfurter":

> I've often wondered what goes into a hot dog. Now I know and I wish I didn't.

**What it shows.** Two short sentences. No setup, no "have you ever wondered…",
no context paragraph. Surprise plus a hint of the writer's voice. The reader has
to read sentence three. That's the whole job of a lead.

---

## 4. The lead: building atmosphere with concrete detail

Joan Didion, opening of "7000 Romaine, Los Angeles 38":

> Seven Thousand Romaine Street is in that part of Los Angeles familiar to
> admirers of Raymond Chandler and Dashiell Hammett: the underside of Hollywood,
> south of Sunset Boulevard, a middle-class slum of "model studios" and
> warehouses and two-family bungalows. Because Paramount and Columbia and Desilu
> and the Samuel Goldwyn studios are nearby, many of the people who live around
> here have some tenuous connection with the motion-picture industry. They once
> processed fan photographs, say, or knew Jean Harlow's manicurist.

**What it shows.** Didion builds momentum by accumulating specific, slightly
faded details. Nothing told, everything shown. The reader doesn't know yet what
the article is about and doesn't care — the atmosphere has already done the
contract.

## 5. The science lead: sequential, one fact at a time

From a _New York Times_ science article by Harold M. Schmeck Jr., quoted by
Zinsser as an example of the upside-down pyramid:

> WASHINGTON — There was a chimpanzee in California with a talent for playing
> ticktacktoe. Its trainers were delighted with this evidence of learning, but
> they were even more impressed by something else. They found they could tell
> from the animal's brain whether any particular move would be right or wrong.
> It depended on the chimpanzee's state of attention. When the trained animal
> was properly attentive, he made the right move.

Zinsser's comment: each subsequent paragraph then answers the next question the
reader would naturally ask. _Why is it page-one news? Hadn't this been possible
before? How is it a new step? What's the purpose?_

**What it shows.** A technical lead doesn't have to be technical. Start with the
one concrete fact the reader can hold in their head, then broaden one sentence
at a time. The reader is escorted through the territory; no leaps.

## 6. Description with precision: bats and echo-location

Diane Ackerman, quoted by Zinsser:

> It's not hard to understand echo-location if you picture bats as calling or
> whistling to their prey with high-frequency sounds. Most of us can't hear
> these. At our youngest and keenest of ear, we might detect sounds of 20,000
> vibrations a second, but bats can vocalize at up to 200,000. They do it not in
> a steady stream but at intervals — 20 or 30 times a second. A bat listens for
> the sounds to return to it, and when the echoes start coming faster and louder
> it knows that the insect it's stalking has flown nearer. By judging the time
> between echoes, a bat can tell how fast the prey is moving and in which
> direction. Some bats are sensitive enough to register a beetle walking on
> sand, and some can detect the movement of a moth flexing its wings as it sits
> on a leaf.

**What it shows.** Hard science, explained in plain English. Specific numbers
(20,000; 200,000; 20 or 30 times a second) anchor the reader. The final two
examples — beetle on sand, moth flexing wings — turn data into image.
Sequential, never showing off, never apologetic.

## 7. Ending: a short sentence that lingers

H. L. Mencken on Calvin Coolidge:

> We suffer most, not when the White House is a peaceful dormitory, but when it
> [has] a tin-pot Paul bawling from the roof. Counting out Harding as a cipher
> only, Dr. Coolidge was preceded by one World Saver and followed by two more.
> What enlightened American, having to choose between any of them and another
> Coolidge, would hesitate for an instant? There were no thrills while he
> reigned, but neither were there any headaches. He had no ideas, and he was not
> a nuisance.

**What it shows.** "He had no ideas, and he was not a nuisance." Five short
words, one comma, perfect rhythm. The ending lands because everything before it
earned it, and because Mencken stopped exactly where he should — no summing up,
no "in conclusion." When the point is made, the curtain drops.

## 8. Famous opening sentences (variety, not formula)

Zinsser cites these as proof that there is no one shape of a great opener:

> In the beginning God created heaven and earth. — _The Bible_

> Put this puzzle together and you will find milk and eggs, meat, fish, beans
> and cereals, greens, fruits and root vegetables — foods that contain our
> essential daily needs. — _Joy of Cooking_, Irma S. Rombauer

> Within five minutes, or ten minutes, no more than that, three of the others
> had called her on the telephone to ask if she had heard that something had
> happened out there. — _The Right Stuff_, Tom Wolfe

> You know more than you think you do. — _Baby and Child Care_, Benjamin Spock

> The problem lay buried, unspoken, for many years in the minds of American
> women. — _The Feminine Mystique_, Betty Friedan

**What it shows.** Length doesn't matter. What matters is that the sentence
creates a small unanswered question and refuses to let go.

## 9. Voice: hard work disguised as ease (E. B. White)

> I spent several days and nights in mid-September with an ailing pig and I feel
> driven to account for this stretch of time, more particularly since the pig
> died at last, and I lived, and things might easily have gone the other way
> round and none left to do the accounting.

Zinsser notes that this is _not_ effortless prose. The grammar is formal. The
words are plain and precise. The cadence is a poet's. It only _feels_ like
someone on a porch in Maine because every syllable was deliberately placed.

**What it shows.** "Sounding like yourself" is a disciplined act, not a relaxed
one. Casual rhythm comes from rewriting, not from typing fast.

## 10. Voice: the failure mode of forced casual (Zinsser's parody of the same passage)

> Ever stay up late babysitting for a sick porker? Believe you me, a guy can
> lose a heckuva lot of shut-eye. I did that gig for three nights back in
> September and my better half thought I'd lost my marbles. (Just kidding, Pam!)
> Frankly, the whole deal kind of bummed me out. Because, you see, the pig up
> and died on me. To tell you the truth, I wasn't feeling in the pink myself, so
> I suppose it could have been yours truly and not old Porky who kicked the
> bucket. And you can bet your bottom dollar Mr. Pig wasn't going to write a
> book about it!

**What it shows.** The breezy style is harder to read than disciplined plain
prose, not easier. It piles up obstacles — cheap slang, false intimacy, winking
parentheticals — while pretending to remove them. The reader knows when they're
being patronized.

## 11. Type A vs Type B writing (_Writing to Learn_)

Zinsser distinguishes two kinds of writing both worth doing:

- **Type A — explanatory.** Writing to transmit information clearly. The reader
  needs to know X; the writer's job is to ensure X arrives intact.
- **Type B — exploratory.** Writing to discover what you think. The act of
  writing surfaces ideas the writer didn't have when they sat down.

Most technical writing is Type A. But the _first draft_ of even a Type A piece
often serves Type B purposes — it's how you find out what you mean. Rewriting is
where Type B turns into Type A.

**What it shows.** Don't conflate the two. The exploratory draft that helped
_you_ think is not necessarily the explanatory draft the _reader_ needs. The
reader needs the result of your thinking, not the process.

## How to use these examples

When editing a draft, find the example that matches your symptom:

| If your draft…                          | Look at example |
| --------------------------------------- | --------------- |
| Sounds important but says little        | 1, 2            |
| Has a slow, throat-clearing opening     | 3, 4, 8         |
| Drowns the reader in jargon             | 5, 6            |
| Limps to a close, then keeps going      | 7               |
| Reads like a corporate voice, not yours | 9, 10           |
| Tries to do everything in one pass      | 11              |
