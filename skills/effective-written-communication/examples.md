# Effective Written Communication — Examples

Examples drawn from William Zinsser, _On Writing Well_ (25th anniversary
edition, 2001) — chapters on Style, The Audience, Business Writing, and The
Sound of Your Voice. The passages are about letters, speeches, and corporate
communication, but the lessons map directly to email, Slack, PR replies, and any
writing where a person is on the other end.

---

## 1. The same message, written two ways (the two school principals)

Both principals wrote to parents at the start of the school year. Same purpose:
welcome the parents, invite contact, set tone.

**Cold (Dr. Jones):**

> Dear Parent: We have established a special phone communication system to
> provide additional opportunities for parent input. During this year we will
> give added emphasis to the goal of communication and utilize a variety of
> means to accomplish this goal. Your inputs, from the unique position as a
> parent, will help us to plan and implement an educational plan that meets the
> needs of your child. An open dialogue, feedback and sharing of information
> between parents and teachers will enable us to work with your child in the
> most effective manner.

**Warm (Dr. Dawson):**

> Keep informed about what is planned for our children this year and let us know
> about your own questions and about any special needs your child may have. I
> have met many of you in the first few weeks. Please continue to stop in to
> introduce yourself or to talk about Foster. I look forward to a very
> productive year for all of us.

Zinsser's diagnosis: _"In the second paragraph I'm being greeted by a person; in
the first I'm hearing from an educator."_ Same school, same goal, opposite
effect.

**What it shows.** The cold version uses no first-person pronoun, no specific
verbs of action, and a thicket of institutional nouns (_special phone
communication system, open dialogue, feedback and sharing of information_). The
warm version uses _I have met_, _let us know_, _please continue to stop in_, _I
look forward to_. The reader can hear a person on the other end — and is much
more likely to actually call.

**Email/Slack equivalent.** Compare these two messages from a tech lead to their
team:

> Effective immediately, we are implementing enhanced coordination protocols for
> cross-functional alignment on the Q3 roadmap. Stakeholder input is welcomed.

> I want to plan Q3 better than we did Q2. I'll send a draft roadmap Friday —
> tell me what you'd cut or add. Will read everything before I publish.

The first is wallpaper. The second gets responses.

## 2. The breezy failure — when "casual" backfires

Zinsser contrasts E. B. White's disciplined, plain-language opening of his essay
about an ailing pig with a parody of how a "breezy" writer would have done the
same passage:

> Ever stay up late babysitting for a sick porker? Believe you me, a guy can
> lose a heckuva lot of shut-eye. I did that gig for three nights back in
> September and my better half thought I'd lost my marbles. (Just kidding, Pam!)
> Frankly, the whole deal kind of bummed me out. Because, you see, the pig up
> and died on me. To tell you the truth, I wasn't feeling in the pink myself, so
> I suppose it could have been yours truly and not old Porky who kicked the
> bucket. And you can bet your bottom dollar Mr. Pig wasn't going to write a
> book about it!

Zinsser's note: _"In the writer's attempt to ease the reader's journey he has
littered the path with obstacles."_ The breezy style isn't easier to read; it's
harder. The reader feels patronized.

**What it shows in messaging.** "Hey friends 👋 just circling back on this —
hope you're crushing it!! 🚀 wanted to ping you re: that thing from earlier —
totally no rush at all, whenever you have a sec ✨" is the same failure mode in
chat clothing. Six emoji, three softeners, and the actual ask is buried. "Hi —
any update on the migration plan? Asking because the deadline is Friday." is
warmer.

## 3. Hedging that loses the reader (real politicians)

Zinsser collected several:

- President Ford on his economic policy: _"We see nothing but increasingly
  brighter clouds every month."_
- Defense Secretary Caspar Weinberger on a Polish crisis: _"There's continuing
  ground for serious concern and the situation remains serious. The longer it
  remains serious, the more ground there is for serious concern."_
- Elliot Richardson: _"And yet, on balance, affirmative action has, I think,
  been a qualified success."_ — five hedges in 13 words.

**What it shows.** Hedging signals to the reader that you don't believe what
you're saying. In a single short message, a stack of hedges (_I think, sort of,
kind of, perhaps, maybe, just, possibly, a bit_) erases the message entirely.

**Messaging equivalent.** Compare:

> I think maybe we could perhaps consider possibly delaying the release a bit,
> if that's something we might be able to do?

with:

> I'd like to delay the release one week — the auth bug isn't fixed yet.

The second is easier to push back on, easier to agree with, and —
counterintuitively — feels more respectful, because it doesn't make the reader
do the work of figuring out what you actually mean.

## 4. The "I" that warms the institution

Zinsser:

> The way to warm up any institution is to locate the missing "I." Remember: "I"
> is the most interesting element in any story.

He notes that in cold corporate prose, _I_ gets buried under _we, the team,
management, the organization_ — and the human disappears. The reader notices.

**What it shows in chat and email.** "It was felt that the proposed approach
might benefit from further review" doesn't tell the reader _who_ felt it or
_what_ should happen. "I'd like another pair of eyes on this before we ship"
does both, in fewer words, and leaves room for the reader to respond like a
person.

Use _we_ when you genuinely mean a group. Use _I_ the rest of the time. Avoid
_one_ and the abstract passive entirely.

## 5. Plain talk lands — even from a giant institution

Zinsser quotes a New York subway sign:

> If you ride the subway regularly you may have seen signs directing you to
> trains you've never heard of before. These are only new names for very
> familiar trains.

His comment: _"Even a huge municipal bureaucracy can talk to its constituents
humanely."_

**What it shows.** Two short sentences, no jargon, no apology, no hedging. The
signs explain what's happening, why it might confuse the reader, and what the
reader should do (nothing — they're already on the right train). Internal
company announcements about renamed services, restructured teams, or migrated
tools can use this exact pattern.

**Compare to the corporate-IT version:**

> As part of our ongoing initiative to optimize the developer experience,
> certain platform components have been rebranded effective immediately. Please
> refer to the FAQ for further information.

vs.

> We renamed `auth-svc` to `identity` and `notify-svc` to `messaging`. The
> behavior didn't change; you don't have to do anything until your next deploy.
> FAQ: …

## 6. The customer letter that loses customers

Zinsser quotes a real "Dear Customer" letter:

> Effective March 30 we will be migrating our end user order entry and supplies
> referral processing to a new telemarketing center.

His translation, after some work: they have a new 800 number, and the "end user"
is him.

His comment: _"Any organization that won't take the trouble to be both clear and
personal in its writing will lose friends, customers and money. Let me put it
another way for business executives: a shortfall will be experienced in
anticipated profitability."_

**What it shows.** Even in messages where the writer technically delivers the
information, layers of corporate vocabulary can drown the meaning. "We've got a
new phone number for orders — 1-800-…" works better in every measurable way.

## 7. The "I might add" sentence that adds nothing

From Zinsser's list of throat-clearing phrases to delete:

| Phrase                            | Reality                                                            |
| --------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| "I might add"                     | Then add it.                                                       |
| "It should be pointed out"        | Then point it out.                                                 |
| "It is interesting to note that"  | If it's interesting, we'll find out when we read it.               |
| "I just wanted to"                | (delete entirely)                                                  |
| "Quick question:" (when it isn't) | Just ask the question.                                             |
| "Sorry to bother you, but"        | Don't lower your own status before you've asked.                   |
| "Per my last email"               | Resend the relevant part; the framing reads as passive-aggressive. |
| "Hope this email finds you well"  | Conveys nothing; uses up the reader's first impression.            |

**What it shows.** Throat-clearing is the verbal equivalent of standing at
someone's desk waiting to be noticed. In writing, the reader is already reading
— start with the thing you wanted to say.

## 8. Voice rooted in a real person (E. B. White, "The Hen")

> Chickens do not always enjoy an honorable position among city-bred people,
> although the egg, I notice, goes on and on. Right now the hen is in favor. The
> war has deified her and she is the darling of the home front, feted at
> conference tables, praised in every smoking car, her girlish ways and curious
> habits the topic of many an excited husbandryman to whom yesterday she was a
> stranger without honor or allure.

Zinsser's comment: _"There's a man writing about a subject I have absolutely no
interest in. Yet I enjoy this piece thoroughly. I like the simple beauty of its
style. I like the rhythms, the unexpected but refreshing words ('deified,'
'allure,' 'cackling'), the specific details… But mainly what I like is that this
is a man telling me unabashedly about a love affair with poultry that goes back
to 1907."_

**What it shows.** Even in something as small as an internal Slack message,
voice matters. Showing genuine interest in the thing you're writing about — not
performed enthusiasm, just real interest — earns the reader's attention. The
principle scales down: a one-paragraph status update written by someone who
actually cares about the project reads completely differently from one written
by someone who is just doing it because they were asked.

## 9. The Thanksgiving Proclamation — when warmth is exactly the form

Governor Wilbur Cross of Connecticut, 1936:

> Time out of mind at this turn of the seasons when the hardy oak leaves rustle
> in the wind and the frost gives a tang to the air and the dusk falls early and
> the friendly evenings lengthen under the heel of Orion, it has seemed good to
> our people to join together in praising the Creator and Preserver, who has
> brought us by a way that we did not know to the end of another year…

Zinsser called it _"a masterpiece of eloquence"_ and noted: _"Notice how many of
the governor's words are anything but vague: leaves, wind, frost, air, evening,
earth, comforts, soil, labor, breath, body, justice, courage, peace, land,
rites, home."_

**What it shows.** Some messages aren't transactional — they exist to mark a
moment (a project shipping, a teammate leaving, a difficult quarter ending). For
those, warmth is not optional decoration; it _is_ the message. The way warmth
lands is through specific, concrete nouns and the rhythms of plain speech. _Not_
through "I'd like to take this opportunity to acknowledge…" — that's the cold
institutional voice masquerading as warmth.

A team-leaving message in this register might be: _"Maria's last day is Friday.
She wrote the first version of our deploy script in 2019 and she's been the
person who actually understood the build graph ever since. Drop by her desk this
week or send her a note — she's earned every word."_ That's the Wilbur Cross
pattern at human scale: concrete, specific, no formula.

## 10. Credibility runs on belief

Zinsser:

> Sell yourself, and your subject will exert its own appeal. Believe in your own
> identity and your own opinions. Writing is an act of ego, and you might as
> well admit it. Use its energy to keep yourself going.

**What it shows.** In professional communication, the writer who clearly
believes what they're saying is hard to argue with, even when wrong. The writer
who hedges, qualifies, and disclaims is easy to ignore even when right. This
isn't a license to be arrogant — it's a recognition that conviction reads
through the page, and so does its absence. If you don't believe what you're
writing, the reader won't either.

## How to use these examples

When drafting or editing a message, find the failure mode that matches your
symptom:

| If your message…                                   | Look at example |
| -------------------------------------------------- | --------------- |
| Sounds like the company wrote it, not you          | 1, 4, 6         |
| Is doing forced-casual to seem friendly            | 2               |
| Is buried under hedges and softeners               | 3, 7            |
| Erases who is asking for what                      | 4               |
| Is a renaming/restructuring/migration announcement | 5, 6            |
| Is about marking a person or a moment              | 8, 9            |
| Feels like it doesn't quite believe itself         | 10              |
