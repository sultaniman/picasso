# Technical Writing

> **Source:** William Zinsser, _On Writing Well_ (Harper & Row, 1976; multiple
> revised editions through 2006) and _Writing to Learn_ (1988). The canonical
> references for clear nonfiction prose in English.

## Core principle

**Writing is thinking on paper.** Muddled prose is muddled thinking — there is
no "I know what I mean, I just can't write it." If the sentence is fuzzy, the
thought is fuzzy. Fix the thought.

**The reader knows nothing.** Not "less than you." Nothing. They have a
30-second attention span and a stack of competing distractions. Every sentence
must earn the next one.

## The four tenets

1. **Clarity** — one meaning per sentence; the reader should never have to
   re-read
2. **Simplicity** — the shortest word that does the job; no Latinate inflation
3. **Brevity** — most first drafts can be cut by 50% without losing information
4. **Humanity** — written by a person, for a person; not a generated artifact

## Before drafting — set unities

Decide once, then hold:

| Unity   | Question                                                     |
| ------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Pronoun | First person (`I`/`we`)? Second (`you`)? Third (impersonal)? |
| Tense   | Past, present, or hypothetical — what's the primary one?     |
| Mood    | Casual, formal, ironic, earnest?                             |
| Scope   | What ONE thing should the reader take away?                  |

Reduce before you write. _Moby-Dick_ is one man, one whale. Decide what corner
of the subject you'll bite off, and let everything else go.

## The lead

The most important sentence is the first. If it doesn't pull the reader to the
second, the piece is dead.

- Hook with freshness, paradox, an unusual fact, a question, or a concrete scene
- State why the reader should keep reading — but don't dwell
- Avoid "have-in-common" leads, future archaeologist openings, and every other
  formula
- The next paragraph must do real work, not just decorate

## The body — sequential logic

For technical material, think **upside-down pyramid**: start with the one fact
the reader must know before learning anything else. Each sentence broadens the
previous one. No leaps, no implied bridges.

After every paragraph, ask: _what will the reader want to know next?_ Answer
that, in order, until done.

## The ending

When you've made your point, **stop**. No "in summary," no recapping. The
perfect ending takes the reader slightly by surprise yet feels inevitable —
often a return to a note struck at the beginning, a vivid quotation, or a single
short sentence.

## Sentence-level craft

- **Active verbs.** "Joe saw him" beats "He was seen by Joe." Verbs carry
  sentences.
- **Short Anglo-Saxon words** over long Latinate ones. _Help_ not _facilitate_;
  _use_ not _utilize_; _now_ not _at the present time_.
- **Cut adverbs and adjectives** that duplicate the verb or noun. _Blare_
  already means loud. A daffodil is already yellow.
- **Kill little qualifiers** — _a bit, sort of, rather, quite, very, pretty
  much_. They whittle away the reader's trust.
- **Concept nouns kill prose.** "The current campus hostility is a symptom of
  change" → "Students are angry." Find the person and the verb.
- **Short paragraphs.** Long blocks discourage the reader before they start. But
  don't go below 2–3 sentences — telegraphic paragraphing is condescending.
- **Mood-changers early.** "But," "however," "still," "yet," "instead" — put
  them at the start of the sentence so the reader pivots immediately.

## Voice

You are the commodity. Don't borrow a style. Don't dress up for the page. Write
in the first person when you can; when you can't, still write like _one person_
talking to _one person_. If you wouldn't say "moreover" out loud, don't write
it.

**Avoid breeziness** (forced casual, "believe me," winks, exclamation points).
Effortlessness is a disciplined act — the grammar is formal, the words are
plain, the cadences are tight.

## Rewriting is the work

Rewriting isn't punishment — it's the craft. Most professional sentences pass
through three to five drafts. After the first draft:

1. **Read it aloud.** You'll hear what's clunky.
2. **Bracket every word doing no work** — every "personal friend," "very," "in a
   sense," "as it were." If removing it doesn't change meaning, cut it.
3. **For each sentence, ask: do I need this at all?** The fastest fix is usually
   deletion.
4. **Ask: am I saying it once?** Don't restate the previous sentence in
   different words.
5. **Then look for clarity.** Will a first-time reader follow the logic without
   rereading?

## Quick checks before publishing

- [ ] Lead earns the second sentence
- [ ] One central point, stated clearly
- [ ] No clutter words (`currently`, `at this point in time`,
      `it should be noted`, `in order to`)
- [ ] Active verbs dominate
- [ ] Paragraphs are visually inviting
- [ ] No mid-piece shift in pronoun, tense, or mood
- [ ] Ending lands; nothing after it
- [ ] Read aloud — sentences flow, rhythm varies, nothing trips the tongue

## Common failure modes

| Symptom                                   | Real problem                                      |
| ----------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------- |
| Reader has to re-read a sentence          | The thought wasn't clear when written             |
| Prose feels "important" or "professional" | Inflation — long words substituted for short ones |
| Lots of `is`, `are`, `there were`         | Verb-poor; concept nouns hiding the action        |
| Many qualifiers and hedges                | Writer doesn't believe what they're saying        |
| Mid-piece pivot to brochure tone          | Material is controlling the writer; unity broke   |
| Ending feels like landing gear grinding   | Author kept writing past the natural stop         |
