# Effective Written Communication

> **Lineage:** answer-first structure from Barbara Minto's _The Pyramid
> Principle_ (1973). Reader-respect and clutter discipline from William
> Zinsser's _On Writing Well_ (1976). Tone calibration draws on the
> business-writing tradition more broadly — no single authority for that side.

## Core principle

**You are talking to one person.** Not an audience, not a team, not a
"stakeholder group" — one person reading on their phone between meetings. Write
like you'd talk to them across a desk.

**Two jobs in every message:** convey the information, and protect the
relationship. A message that nails the first and breaks the second has failed.
So has a warm message that buries the ask.

## The shape of an effective message

1. **The point, up front.** What you want them to know, do, or decide — in the
   first sentence or two.
2. **The reason, briefly.** Why this, why now.
3. **The ask, explicit.** "Could you review by Thursday?" not "let me know your
   thoughts."
4. **A close that matches the tone.** Warm if the relationship calls for warmth;
   brisk if you're both busy and they prefer brisk.

You can absolutely add warmth, context, and personality — but _after_ the reader
knows what the message is about. Front-loading pleasantries before the ask makes
the reader scroll.

## When to be warm, when to be tight

| Situation                              | Lean                                  | Why                                              |
| -------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ |
| Quick async question between teammates | Tight, friendly tone, no preamble     | Both are busy; relationship is established       |
| Reaching out to someone new            | Warmer, more context                  | They owe you nothing; earn the read              |
| Bad news / pushback / saying no        | Warm in tone, direct in substance     | Softness in the verbs, clarity in the message    |
| Thanks, congratulations, condolences   | Warm, specific, not formulaic         | "Generic warm" reads as cold                     |
| Status update to a wider audience      | Tight, structured                     | Many readers, few will read carefully            |
| Customer/external reply                | Warm opener, clear answer, warm close | They're judging the company through this message |
| Difficult feedback in writing          | Warmth + precision; never sarcasm     | Tone in writing skews harsher than intended      |

When unsure, write it tight first, then add the warmth you want. Adding warmth
to a clear message is easy. Excavating a clear message from a sea of
pleasantries is hard.

## Voice rules

- **Write in the first person.** "I think we should…" is warmer and more
  accountable than "It is suggested that we…". Use "we" when you genuinely mean
  a group.
- **Sound like yourself.** Don't impersonate the company voice, the boss's
  voice, or LinkedIn voice. The reader can hear it.
- **Avoid hedge-stacks.** "I just sort of wanted to maybe check in if possible."
  Pick one softener or none.
- **Avoid forced casual.** "Hey friends 👋 hope you're crushing it!" reads worse
  than "Hi all,". Effortless warmth is built from plain words in good order, not
  from emoji and exclamation points.
- **Match register to the recipient.** A two-line Slack reply to a peer is
  different from a three-paragraph email to a senior leader you don't know well.

## Openers and closers

| Don't                                          | Do                                                          | Why                                                     |
| ---------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- |
| "I hope this email finds you well."            | "Quick question about X."                                   | The first conveys nothing; the second saves their time  |
| "Just circling back…"                          | "Following up on Thursday's question about X — any update?" | Specific beats vague; no apology needed                 |
| "Sorry to bother you, but…"                    | "Could you help with X when you have a few minutes?"        | Don't open by lowering your own status                  |
| "Per my last email…"                           | (just resend the relevant part)                             | Passive-aggressive; everyone hears it                   |
| "Thanks in advance!" (when asking for a favor) | "Thanks — happy to return the favor."                       | The first puts pressure; the second is reciprocal       |
| "Hope that helps!"                             | "Let me know if anything's unclear."                        | The second invites follow-up; the first closes the door |

## Saying hard things

Direct does not mean cold. Cold loses the relationship; vague loses the message.
Aim for both.

**Pattern for hard feedback in writing:**

- Lead with the substance, not the framing. ("I don't think we should ship
  Friday.")
- One short sentence on the why.
- The action you want them to consider.
- A note acknowledging their position, if it's genuine and you've heard it.

**Pattern for saying no:**

- A clean _no_, not "I'm not sure if I'll be able to…".
- One sentence on why (no over-justification — it sounds like negotiation).
- An offer of what you _can_ do, if you can offer something.

Don't apologize three times for saying no. The reader will respect a clean no;
they'll lose trust in three apologies.

## When warmth helps the message land

Some messages aren't transactional — they're about acknowledging a person. Here,
what you'd cut from a technical doc is the whole point:

- **Thank-yous land when specific.** "Thank you for staying late on the
  migration — I know you had plans that night" beats "Thanks for your hard
  work."
- **Condolences land when human.** No "please reach out if you need anything" —
  offer one concrete thing.
- **Welcomes land when curious.** Ask one real question; don't list ten company
  facts.
- **Apologies land when undefended.** "I was wrong — here's what I'll do" beats
  "I'm sorry if anyone felt…".

Don't trade specificity for length. Three honest sentences land better than a
paragraph of well-meaning fog.

## Things to keep out

- **Buzzwords.** _Synergy, leverage, circle back, ping, touch base, run it up
  the flagpole, low-hanging fruit, bandwidth, EOD_ (unless you mean a literal
  end of day in a clear timezone). Replace with what you actually mean.
- **Throat-clearing.** _I might add. It should be pointed out. As you may be
  aware. With the possible exception of._ If it should be pointed out, point it
  out.
- **Bombast.** "Per our SLA-aligned policy framework" → "We don't refund after
  30 days, sorry."
- **Performative tentativeness in your own area.** If you're the engineer who
  knows the system, "I might be wrong, but maybe we should perhaps consider…"
  wastes everyone's time. State your view.
- **Performative confidence outside your area.** If you don't know, say you
  don't know.
- **Exclamation points as default punctuation.** Save them for actual surprise
  or delight. "Sounds good." reads warm if the context is warm; "Sounds good!!!"
  reads anxious.
- **Capital-letter shouting and ALL-CAPS section headers in chat.** Distracting;
  the reader doesn't need them.

## Sound check before sending

Read it aloud (or in your head with cadence). Ask:

- [ ] If they only read the first sentence, do they know what this is about?
- [ ] Is the ask explicit? Could a tired person miss it?
- [ ] Does the tone match the relationship and the situation?
- [ ] Did I open by lowering my status without reason ("Sorry to bother…")?
- [ ] Did I bury a hard thing under softeners?
- [ ] Is there anything that could be misread as sarcasm or judgment?
      (Especially in chat.)
- [ ] Would I be happy to receive this message exactly as written?

## On longer messages

Sometimes the right message is long — a project recap, a thoughtful
disagreement, a customer escalation reply. In that case:

- **Open with a tl;dr.** One or two sentences. The rest is for the reader who
  wants the full picture.
- **Sub-headers if it's over ~300 words.** Skimmability matters.
- **Concrete details over abstract claims.** "We hit 2.3s p99 latency last week"
  beats "performance has been a concern."
- **End with the action.** What you want the reader to do, decide, or know is
  decided.

A long message that earns its length feels respectful. A long message that's
long because the writer didn't edit feels burdensome. The difference is
rewriting.

## When the medium changes the rules

- **Email:** Subject line carries weight; treat it as the lead. "Decision
  needed: Postgres vs. MySQL by Thursday" beats "Update".
- **Slack/chat:** Shorter, more emoji-tolerant, multi-message OK. But don't
  split a single thought across six messages — it disrupts the reader. Use
  threads.
- **PR comments:** Distinguish blocking from non-blocking. "nit:", "question:",
  and "blocking:" prefixes save back-and-forth.
- **Public posts (changelogs, announcements):** Tighter than chat. Every word is
  read by many; rewrite twice.
- **Difficult conversations:** Prefer voice or video over text when the topic is
  emotionally loaded. Writing strips intonation; written messages skew harsher.

## Failure patterns

| Symptom                                               | Underlying issue                                   |
| ----------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------- |
| Reader replies asking "what do you need from me?"     | Ask wasn't explicit                                |
| Long thread of misunderstandings                      | First message buried the point under context       |
| Reader takes message harsher than intended            | Forgot that text strips tone — needed a warmer cue |
| Reply feels formal in a casual relationship           | Borrowed corporate voice instead of your own       |
| Reader doesn't reply                                  | Either the ask was unclear or there wasn't one     |
| You wrote three paragraphs and now don't want to send | Probably one paragraph; rewrite                    |
